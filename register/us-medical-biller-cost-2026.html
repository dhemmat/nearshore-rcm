The fully-loaded cost of a US medical biller in 2026.

ToUS healthcare operators — practice administrators, RCM leaders, CFOs
FromThe NearshoreRCM founding team
ReWhat one billing seat actually costs — the number behind the salary number
DateAug 24, 2026 · benchmarks reviewed quarterly

Ask what a medical biller costs and you'll hear a salary: around $42,700 on the national average, with starting ranges of $39,500–$50,000 depending on market and experience. That number is real — and it is not what a billing seat costs you. The true figure is the fully-loaded cost: salary plus employment load, recruiting, equipment, and the turnover tax nobody budgets for. Here is the honest math, line by line, with sources.

Line 1 — Base salary

National averages for the core revenue cycle seats in 2026: medical billers around $42,700 (ZipRecruiter), with staffing-firm starting ranges of $39,500–$50,000 (Robert Half 2026 Salary Guide). Experienced billing specialists average roughly $54,300. Adjacent seats: prior authorization specialists $43,500–$50,400; credentialing specialists starting at $43,750–$57,000; certified coders (CPC) $65,000–$67,000, with multi-credentialed coders above $71,000 (AAPC). The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median for the broader medical records specialists category at $50,250, and medical and health services managers — the category covering RCM managers — at a $117,960 median.

Line 2 — The employment load

Salary is what the employee sees; load is what you pay. Employer FICA adds 7.65%. Health insurance, retirement match, paid time off, and statutory costs push the total premium over base to a commonly used 20–30% for full-time staff. On a $42,700 biller, a mid-range 25% load adds roughly $10,700 — before anyone has processed a claim.

Line 3 — Recruiting and equipment

Every seat carries acquisition and tooling costs: job advertising or agency fees, interviewing time, onboarding, a workstation, software licenses. Amortized conservatively, this runs on the order of $1,500 per seat per year — more in the year you actually hire, and much more if you use a recruiter at typical contingency rates.

Line 4 — The turnover tax

This is the line that never appears in the budget and always appears in the A/R report. When a biller leaves, the position sits open while you recruit, then a new hire ramps for weeks to months. During that window, claims still go out — but rejections queue, denials age past appeal deadlines, and follow-up calls don't happen. Operators consistently describe three to six months of degraded A/R per departure. Unworked denials are pure loss: the industry sees roughly 10–15% of claims denied on first submission, and denials that miss timely-filing windows are unrecoverable at any staffing level.

The arithmetic, per seat, per year: $42,700 base × 1.25 load ≈ $53,400 + $1,500 recruiting/equipment ≈ $55,000 — before turnover risk. Add realistic turnover exposure and senior-seat mix, and the honest planning figure for a US billing seat is $58,000–$75,000+ per year. A four-seat team (two billers, a denials specialist, a certified coder) benchmarks around $254,000 annually at national-average rates — $1.27M over five years.

What operators do with this number

Three rational responses exist. Some organizations absorb it — for a small practice with one tenured biller, that can be right. Some shrink the function and accept unworked denial queues — which is a decision to donate revenue to payers on a schedule. And a growing number restructure where the work is performed: the tasks are digital, procedural, and time-zone-sensitive, which is precisely the profile that nearshore delivery handles well. Teams in Bogotá and Santo Domingo work US Eastern hours — payer calls happen live, not overnight — at a substantially lower fully-loaded cost per seat, with employment, retention, and replacement carried by the vendor rather than the practice.
