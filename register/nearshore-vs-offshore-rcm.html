Nearshore vs. offshore for revenue cycle work: the time-zone math.

ToUS healthcare operators — practice administrators, RCM leaders, CFOs
FromThe NearshoreRCM founding team
ReWhy overlap hours decide RCM outsourcing outcomes more than hourly rates do
DateAug 24, 2026

Every outsourcing comparison starts with the hourly rate, and the hourly rate is the wrong first question. Revenue cycle work is time-zone-sensitive in a way most back-office work is not, because the counterparty — the payer — answers phones roughly 8am to 5pm in US time zones, and a meaningful share of revenue cycle work only moves when a human at the payer is on the line. Here is the math that actually governs outcomes.

The overlap table

Bogotá runs on GMT−5 with no daylight saving — which means it matches US Eastern Time exactly for most of the year and sits within an hour of it always. Santo Domingo runs GMT−4 year-round: identical to Eastern in summer, one hour ahead in winter. A team in either city works your business day as their business day. Now the alternative: Manila is 12–13 hours ahead of Eastern; most of India is 9.5–10.5 hours ahead. For an offshore team to touch a payer during payer business hours, someone works the night shift — permanently.

What actually requires live hours

Not everything. Charge entry, payment posting from ERAs, and a good portion of coding are asynchronous — they can be done at midnight and be exactly as good. But look at where revenue actually leaks: denial follow-up calls, claim-status calls that the portal can't answer, eligibility questions before a same-day add-on visit, peer-to-peer scheduling for prior auths, appeals that need one clarifying answer from a payer rep, and the constant small escalations between your in-house staff and the outsourced team. All of that is synchronous. All of it happens 8-to-5 Eastern or it doesn't happen that day.

The latency tax

A denial that needs three clarifying exchanges — one with the payer, two with your front office — closes the same day when everyone shares business hours. Put a 12-hour offset in the loop and each exchange costs a day: the same denial now takes three to four days to resolve. Multiply that by a queue of hundreds, against payer appeal deadlines that do not extend, and the latency tax converts directly into aged A/R and expired appeals.

The second-order cost is quality of supervision. Offshore night shifts carry chronically higher turnover, and your managers can either supervise the work live (by working nights themselves) or review it the morning after (adding another day of latency to every correction). Neither is free.

Where offshore honestly wins

We build nearshore teams, so read this knowing where we stand — but the honest version of this comparison includes the cases we lose. If your outsourced scope is purely asynchronous volume — data entry, records processing, some coding pipelines — and price is the only axis that matters, offshore delivery at the lowest possible rate can be a rational choice. The mistake we see is organizations pricing the whole revenue cycle as if it were that kind of work, then discovering that the synchronous third of it — the third where the money actually leaks — has been running on a one-day delay.

The questions to ask any vendor

  • During which US hours is my team actually at their desks — not "coverage," desks?
  • Who calls payers, and when? Show me call logs by hour.
  • What is the turnaround on a question my office manager asks at 2pm Eastern?
  • Who supervises the people doing my work, and what shift does that supervisor work?

Time zones are geography; they don't negotiate. Our teams in Bogotá and Santo Domingo work Eastern hours because they live in Eastern-adjacent longitudes — no night shift, no latency tax, no heroics required to answer a 2pm question at 2pm.
