How to structure a denials team that actually recovers money.

ToUS healthcare operators — practice administrators, RCM leaders, CFOs
FromThe NearshoreRCM founding team
ReDenial management as a designed function, not a side task — roles, queues, cadence, metrics
DateAug 24, 2026

Across the industry, roughly 10–15% of claims are denied on first submission. What separates organizations is not the denial rate — it's what happens next. In most billing departments, denials are worked "when there's time," by billers whose productivity is measured on claims submitted. There is never time. The denials age quietly past appeal deadlines, and the write-off happens by default, invisibly, without anyone ever deciding it. A denials function that recovers money is a designed thing. Here is the design.

The roles

At scale, denial management splits into four kinds of work, and they are different jobs: triage — every denial sorted by reason code and appeal deadline the day it lands; payer contact — the calls that resolve the third of denials that are payer error or missing information; appeals — written, documented, filed against the clock; and root cause — the pattern analysis that routes fixes to the front of the process so the same denial stops recurring. A smaller practice runs all four in one senior person with a protected calendar; a group at volume staffs them as distinct seats under a lead who owns the trend line. What never works is spreading the work thin across people measured on something else.

The queue discipline

First-in-first-out is the wrong order. Work the queue by appeal deadline first, dollar value second. Timely-filing and appeal windows are unforgiving: Medicare allows twelve months from the date of service for initial claims, while commercial payer windows are set by contract and commonly run anywhere from 90 days to a year — some shorter. A $3,000 surgical denial with 20 days left on its window outranks everything else on the desk. If your work queue can't sort by deadline, that's the first fix.

The cadence

  • Daily: new denials triaged and coded by reason; deadline-critical items worked first.
  • Weekly: root-cause review — which reason codes are growing, which payer changed behavior, what goes back to the front desk, coding, or credentialing to fix at the source.
  • Monthly: payer scorecard — denial rate, overturn rate, and days-to-pay by payer, because payers respond to patterns presented with data.

The metrics that matter

Four numbers tell you whether the function works: overturn rate (of denials appealed, how many paid), days-to-appeal (denial receipt to appeal filed — the deadline-risk gauge), preventable share (what fraction of denials trace to fixable front-end causes), and the one almost nobody tracks: worked rate — the percentage of denials that received any action at all before write-off. That last number is where the silent losses hide.

The silent write-off math: (monthly claim volume) × (denial rate) × (share never worked) × (average claim value) = the number nobody puts in the board deck. Run it once with your own figures. Organizations that do rarely need further convincing that denials deserve dedicated seats.

Why this is a staffing problem

Every failure mode above is, underneath, a capacity problem: triage doesn't happen daily because nobody owns it; appeals miss windows because the biller had submissions to hit; root cause never runs because it's nobody's Tuesday. A dedicated denials seat — or a pod with a lead — exists precisely so this work is somebody's whole job, measured on recovery, during the payer's business hours.

Put dedicated seats on your denial queue.
